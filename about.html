About Me

From building machines to studying the people who use them

Engineer by training, human-factors researcher by direction — I work where apparatus, data, and human experience meet.

I am a research assistant at the School of Psychology, Shenzhen University, where I study motion sickness — predicting susceptibility from DTI-derived brain networks with machine learning, and examining how predictability cues and non-driving-related tasks interact in automated driving. My daily work spans EEG experiments, VR environment development, and the six-axis motion platform our studies run on.

Before psychology, I spent five years as a mechanical engineer: a bachelor's degree ranked 1st of 194 at Dongguan University of Technology, then a master's at The Chinese University of Hong Kong, where I built a YOLOv8 + DeepSORT pipeline for tracking micro-robot swarms, and an internship at the CAS Hong Kong Centre for AI & Robotics, where I worked on teleoperated robotic arms and a patented IMU motion-capture glove. Somewhere between calibrating a robot's eye and designing a glove that reads human fingers, I realized the questions I cared most about were not about the machines — they were about the people operating them.

That realization has a practical edge: psychology research on technology needs people who can build the technology. Motion-sickness experiments need VR environments and motion platforms; neural-data studies need reproducible pipelines. My goal for a PhD is human factors and human–technology interaction — continuing to study how humans respond in complex systems, with both the experimental methods of psychology and the engineering ability to build whatever the experiment requires.
